The frontier never disappears

I used to worry about AI and the future of work, but now I keep returning to one conviction: the frontier never disappears. It only moves.

I pursued software engineering because it felt like the frontier technology of my time. But what is technology, really? It is the set of things humanity has not fully figured out yet.

Once upon a time, wheels, taps, engines, electricity, antibiotics, and the internet were all frontier technologies. Then they became infrastructure. The miracle became ordinary.

That is the pattern.

Today, I believe biology is one of the ultimate frontiers.

Biology is full of unsolved problems: disease, aging, regeneration, cognition, food systems, diagnostics, drug discovery, human performance. It is messy, complex, probabilistic, and deeply physical.

It is exactly the kind of domain where better tools, better models, and better systems thinking can unlock enormous progress.

Imagine if biology can move with even a fraction of the iteration speed of software! Imagine faster experiments, cheaper diagnostics, personalized treatments, programmable therapies, and biological systems we can understand with the same clarity we expect from code.

That is what keeps me optimistic.

Engineers will never run out of problems to solve. Basic Economics 101 teaches that human wants are unlimited. I think the same is true of human problems.

Computer science gave me more than a career. It gave me a way of thinking: break problems down, model systems, search the space, test assumptions, iterate, optimize, and build. Those tools are portable. They do not belong only to software. They belong anywhere complexity exists.

So I am not trying to cling to a shrinking definition of software engineering. I am trying to stay at the edge of useful work.

The frontier is moving. Good.

That means there is still work worth doing.
